Wire Gauge Calculator

Select the right wire gauge (AWG) based on current draw and cable length to minimize voltage drop.

Understanding Wire Gauge Calculations

This calculator helps you select the appropriate wire gauge (AWG - American Wire Gauge) for your UAV power distribution system. Proper wire sizing ensures minimal voltage drop, safe operation, and efficient power delivery.

Wire Resistance

Wire resistance depends on the material, cross-sectional area, length, and temperature. For copper wire at 20°C:

Resistance Calculation R = ρ × L / A

where ρ is resistivity (material-dependent), L is the total wire length (round-trip: 2 × cable length), and A is the cross-sectional area. The calculator uses standardized AWG resistance values per meter.

Temperature Correction

Resistance increases with temperature:

Temperature Coefficient R(T) = R₀ × (1 + α × ΔT)

where R₀ is resistance at 20°C, α ≈ 0.00393/°C for copper, and ΔT is the temperature difference from 20°C.

Voltage Drop

Voltage drop occurs due to current flowing through wire resistance:

Voltage Drop Calculation V_drop = I × R

Expressed as a percentage: V_drop% = (V_drop / V_system) × 100. Typical UAV systems target less than 3-5% voltage drop to maintain efficiency and avoid brownouts under load.

Power Loss

Power dissipated as heat in the wire:

Power Loss Calculation P_loss = I² × R

This represents wasted energy. Lower resistance wire reduces power loss and improves overall system efficiency.

Wire Selection Criteria

The calculator recommends the smallest (highest AWG number) wire that satisfies:

  • Voltage drop threshold: Keeps voltage drop below your specified limit
  • Current capacity (ampacity): Wire can safely handle the continuous current
  • Thermal margin: Prevents overheating during normal operation

Limitations

  • DC assumptions: Calculations assume DC current. AC systems have additional considerations like skin effect and inductance at high frequencies.
  • Ampacity ratings: Values are conservative estimates for open-air installation. Bundled wires or enclosed installations may require derating. Always check manufacturer specifications.
  • Connector losses: The model doesn't account for voltage drop at connectors (XT60, XT90, etc.), which can be significant. Use high-quality connectors rated for your current.
  • Wire quality: Calculations assume pure copper or aluminum. Copper-clad aluminum (CCA) or low-quality wire has higher resistance than specified.
  • Safety margins: For critical applications or sustained high currents, consider oversizing by one or two gauge sizes for additional safety margin.
